Which antiseptic works best? Zones of inhibition

Spread bacteria over the agar, add paper discs soaked in different antiseptics, incubate, then measure the clear zones where bacteria could not grow. The bigger the zone, the more effective the chemical.

Real-lab safety: treat all bacterial cultures as harmful: work near a flame, tape the lid shut, never open it after incubation, and wash hands afterwards. Incubate school cultures at 25 °C, never 37 °C (why? see Q4).

Analysis questions

1. Which disc was most effective against the bacteria? How do you know from your measurements?

The disc with the largest clear zone (usually penicillin in this simulation). A larger zone of inhibition means the chemical diffused into the agar and killed or stopped bacteria further from the disc.

2. Why include a disc soaked in sterile water?

It is the control. It proves that any clear zone is caused by the antiseptic/antibiotic and not by the paper disc or the water itself. The water disc should have no zone.

3. Give two variables that must be kept the same for a fair test.

Same species and amount of bacteria; same size/concentration of discs; same incubation temperature and time; same agar depth. (Any two.)

4. Why are school cultures incubated at 25 °C and not 37 °C?

37 °C is human body temperature — ideal for growing pathogens that infect humans. 25 °C slows growth and makes dangerous human pathogens much less likely to thrive.

Herd immunity: what happens when we vaccinate?

1. Describe the pattern in your data.

The more people vaccinated, the fewer people infected. Above roughly 80–90% vaccinated, outbreaks usually die out almost immediately — there are too few unprotected people for the disease to spread through.

2. How does vaccination protect people who are not vaccinated (e.g. babies, people with weak immune systems)?

Herd immunity. When most people are immune, the pathogen cannot find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so chains of infection break — and even unvaccinated people are unlikely to meet the disease.

3. How does a vaccine create immunity without causing the disease?

A vaccine introduces a weakened or inactive form of the pathogen (or its antigens). Lymphocytes respond by making antibodies and memory cells. If the real pathogen arrives later, the memory cells trigger a fast, strong response.
