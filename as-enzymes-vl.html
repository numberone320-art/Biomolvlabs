How a colorimeter works

White light passes through a coloured filter, then through the solution in a cuvette. A light meter measures how much light gets through. The more strongly the solution absorbs, the higher the absorbance and the higher the concentration of the coloured substance. Click any part to read about it. Check your understanding

1Why is the filter that gives the highest absorbance the best one to use? [2]
The blue-black starch–iodine complex absorbs orange-red light most strongly (it lets blue through, which is why it looks blue). Using that filter gives the largest change in absorbance for a given change in concentration, so the method is most sensitive and small differences are detected.
2Why is the colorimeter zeroed with a blank of water plus iodine, not just water? [2]
The blank contains everything except the starch. Zeroing with it removes the absorbance of the cuvette, the water and the iodine solution itself, so the reading shows only the colour due to the starch–iodine complex. (Try zeroing with plain water on the violet filter to see the iodine's own absorbance.)
3A student holds the cuvette by its clear faces. Explain the effect on the reading. [2]
Fingerprints on the clear faces sit in the light path. They absorb or scatter some light, so less reaches the light meter and the absorbance reads too high, making the concentration seem higher than it is.
4A reading is 25% transmission. Calculate the absorbance. [2]
A = log₁₀(100 ÷ %T) = log₁₀(100 ÷ 25) = log₁₀ 4 = 0.60. Absorbance has no units.
5Why does a calibration curve need the same filter, cuvette and volume of iodine for every tube? [2]
Absorbance depends on the filter wavelength, the path length and the amount of iodine as well as on starch concentration. Keeping them constant means absorbance changes only because concentration changes, so an unknown can be read off the same line.

Practical Activity 3.1 · Measuring the rate of disappearance of a substrate

Amylase hydrolyses starch to maltose. At timed intervals you add a drop of the reaction mixture to iodine solution on a spotting tile. As starch is used up, the colour goes blue-black → brown → pale brown → no blue-black colour left. Time how long it takes to reach the end-point, or measure each sample in the colorimeter to get numbers you can plot.

Safety: wear eye protection. Iodine solution stains skin and is an irritant. Amylase is an enzyme and can cause allergic reactions: avoid skin contact and do not inhale dust.

Constant for every run: 5 cm³ of 1% starch, 5 cm³ of amylase, pH 7 buffer, 1 drop of mixture into 2 drops of iodine.

Check your understanding

1Sketch the curve of starch remaining against time and explain its shape. [3]
The curve starts high and falls steeply at first, then more and more gently, levelling off at zero. At the start there are many starch molecules, so active sites are almost always occupied and the rate is fastest. As starch is used up, collisions between substrate and active sites become less frequent, so the rate falls until no substrate is left.
2How do you use this curve to find the initial rate? [2]
Draw a tangent to the curve at t = 0 and calculate its gradient (change in absorbance ÷ change in time). A quicker estimate is the change in the first 30 s. The tangent is better because the rate is already falling during those 30 s.
3Why are samples tested in separate wells of iodine, not with iodine added to the whole reaction mixture? [1]
Iodine interferes with the reaction and slows it down, so it must not be in the reaction tube.
4Why must both tubes be pre-incubated in the water bath before mixing? [2]
So the starch and amylase are already at the bath temperature when the reaction starts. Otherwise the mixture warms (or cools) during the first minutes and the true temperature of the reaction is unknown. Untick the box and compare runs at 60 °C to see the effect.
5Explain why the rate falls above about 45 °C. [3]
High kinetic energy makes the enzyme molecule vibrate so much that hydrogen and ionic bonds holding its tertiary structure break. The active site changes shape, so starch no longer fits and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form. The enzyme is denatured; this is not reversed on cooling.
6Suggest one advantage of the colorimeter over the spotting tile. [1]
The end-point is judged by eye on the tile, which is subjective. The colorimeter gives a quantitative reading at every time, so you can plot a progress curve and compare runs objectively.

Practical Activity 3.2 · Immobilising enzymes

Lactase is mixed with sodium alginate and dripped into calcium chloride. Each drop instantly becomes a jelly bead with the enzyme trapped inside. Packed into a column, the beads convert lactose in milk into glucose and galactose, and the milk runs out free of both lactose and lactase.

Safety: wear eye protection. Calcium chloride is an irritant. Never taste the milk in a laboratory.

Check your understanding

1Why test the product with glucose test strips rather than Benedict's reagent? [2]
Lactose is itself a reducing sugar, so milk gives a positive Benedict's result before any lactase has acted. Glucose strips respond to glucose only, so they show that hydrolysis has happened.
2Explain why slower flow through the column gives more glucose. [2]
Slower flow means the milk spends longer in contact with the beads, so more lactose molecules collide with active sites of lactase and more are hydrolysed before the milk leaves.
3Why are the beads rinsed with distilled water before use? [2]
To remove excess calcium chloride and any lactase stuck to the outside of the beads. Otherwise free enzyme washes into the first sample, raising its glucose and contaminating the product.
4What is the control column and what should it show? [2]
Beads made in the same way but with water instead of lactase. The milk should contain no glucose, showing that glucose in the test column comes from the enzyme, not from the alginate, the calcium chloride or the milk.
5State three advantages of using immobilised enzymes. [3]
  • The enzyme can be recovered and reused, which cuts cost.
  • The product is not contaminated with enzyme.
  • Immobilised enzymes are more stable and tolerate a wider range of temperature and pH, because the alginate holds the molecule in shape and shields part of it.
6Use the heat-stability graph to compare free and immobilised lactase. [3]
Both keep nearly all their activity at low temperatures. Free lactase loses activity from about 50 °C and is almost fully denatured by about 65 °C. Immobilised lactase keeps its activity to about 10 °C higher. Quote two pairs of values read from your graph to support the comparison.
