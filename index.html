      GUIDE

      Bench controls and food tests

      How the bench works

      • Dosing: drag a bottle onto a tube in the rack (or tap the bottle, then tap the tube). Hold Pour for liquids, tap Add a drop for iodine and DCPIP, tap Add a pinch for sodium hydrogencarbonate.
      • Moving tubes: lift a tube to carry it. Drop it in the beaker to heat, or in the sink to empty and rinse it.
      • Mixing: shake a lifted tube side to side, or tap it.
      • Burner: open the gas tap to light the flame. A yellow flame is a safety flame; opening the air hole gives a hot blue flame. The bath needs about 70 °C. Turn the gas off when you finish.
      • Time: the bath runs on simulated time, so a 3 minute heat takes about 9 seconds.

      Starch: iodine test

      orange-brown → blue-black

      Add 3 drops of iodine solution to about 2 cm³ of sample. Starch (amylose coils) traps iodine and turns the mixture blue-black. No starch: it stays orange-brown.

      Safety: iodine stains skin and clothes.

      Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

      blue → brick-red

      Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's reagent to 2 cm³ of sample and heat in a water bath at 70 °C or hotter for about 3 minutes. Reducing sugars reduce blue Cu²⁺ to red Cu⁺ (copper(I) oxide precipitate). The colour gives a rough idea of concentration.

      Safety: use a water bath, not a direct flame. Hot water scalds.

      Non-reducing sugars (sucrose)

      AS

      Benedict's stays blue with sucrose. Take a fresh sample, add 1 cm³ dilute hydrochloric acid and heat in the bath for about 2 minutes to break the glycosidic bond. Cool, then add sodium hydrogencarbonate a pinch at a time until the fizzing stops, so the mixture is alkaline. Add Benedict's reagent and heat again. A brick-red precipitate shows a non-reducing sugar was present.

      Safety: dilute HCl is an irritant. Add the hydrogencarbonate slowly, it fizzes.

      Protein: biuret test

      blue → purple

      Add 2 cm³ of biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide plus a little copper(II) sulfate). Cu²⁺ ions form a purple complex with peptide bonds. In this lab the two solutions are combined in one bottle.

      Safety: sodium hydroxide is corrosive.

      Lipids: ethanol emulsion test

      clear → cloudy white

      Add 2 cm³ of ethanol to the sample and shake so any lipid dissolves. Then add 3 cm³ of water. Lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets, an emulsion that looks cloudy white. No lipid: the mixture stays clear. Order matters: ethanol first, shake, then water.

      Safety: ethanol is flammable. Turn the burner off before you open the ethanol, and never put an ethanol tube in the bath.

      Vitamin C: DCPIP test

      blue → colourless

      Vitamin C is a reducing agent: it reduces blue DCPIP to a colourless form. In the lab you normally add juice drop by drop to DCPIP until it turns colourless, so less juice means more vitamin C. On this bench the tube already holds the sample. Add DCPIP drop by drop and count how many lose their blue colour. More drops decolourised means more vitamin C.
