Think like a microscopist

1. Total magnification = eyepiece × objective. The eyepiece here is ×10. What is the total magnification with the ×40 objective?

×10 × ×40 = ×400. The image is 400 times larger than the real specimen.

2. What happens to the number of cells you can see as you switch from ×4 to ×40?

You see fewer cells, but larger. Higher magnification = smaller field of view. This is why you always start on low power to find the specimen, then zoom in.

3. Which knob (coarse or fine) should you use with the ×40 objective — and why?

Only the fine focus knob. At high power the objective is very close to the slide; the coarse knob could push the lens through the coverslip and crack it.

Measuring cell size with an eyepiece graticule

The circle now shows the view at ×100 total with a ruler (graticule) across it: 0 to 100 eyepiece units. Calibration: at this magnification, 100 eyepiece units = 1 mm (1000 µm), so 1 unit = 10 µm.

Look at the eyepiece view (set to ×10 objective = ×100 total). One onion cell spans about 10 eyepiece units.

1. Length of one cell = units  

2. Convert to micrometres (1 unit = 10 µm): µm  

One cell ≈ 10 units (accept 9–11 — estimating to the nearest unit is normal).
10 units × 10 µm = 100 µm. A typical onion epidermis cell is about 100–400 µm long.

µm = micrometre. 1000 µm = 1 mm. Cells are measured in µm because they are far too small for millimetres.

Magnification calculation

A student draws one cell. The drawing is 50 mm wide. The real cell is 100 µm wide.

Step 1 — same units: 50 mm = µm  

Step 2 — magnification = image ÷ actual =  

50 mm × 1000 = 50 000 µm.
M = 50 000 ÷ 100 = ×500. Remember: magnification = image size ÷ actual size — with both in the same units.
