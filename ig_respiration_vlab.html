EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 12.1

Measuring the rate of respiration with a respirometer

Germinating seeds respire fast. They take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide — but the potassium hydroxide (KOH) absorbs the CO₂, so the gas volume inside falls and the coloured droplet creeps along the capillary tube. Measure how far it moves and calculate the rate.

Real-lab safety: KOH is corrosive — handle the pellets with forceps and wear eye protection.

rate = distance moved ÷ time. (Multiplying by the capillary’s cross-section area would give volume of O₂ per minute.)

1. Why does the droplet move towards the flask?

The seeds remove O₂ from the air and release CO₂, but the KOH absorbs the CO₂. Total gas volume falls → pressure falls → air (and the droplet) is drawn towards the flask.

2. Why use boiled seeds as a control?

Boiling kills the seeds, so no respiration — the droplet should not move. If it does move slightly, that change is due to temperature or pressure changes, and you subtract it from the live-seed result.

3. Predict: what happens to the rate if you warm the respirometer from 20 °C to 30 °C? Why?

It increases (roughly doubles). Respiration is controlled by enzymes, and enzyme reactions speed up with temperature — up to the optimum.
EXPERIMENTAL SKILLS 12.2

Anaerobic respiration in yeast: effect of temperature

Yeast can respire without oxygen (anaerobically): it breaks down glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Count the CO₂ bubbles produced in 30 seconds at each temperature, then decide what temperature yeast likes best.

aerobic: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ lots of energy released)
anaerobic in yeast: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (+ less energy released)
anaerobic in muscles: glucose → lactic acid (+ less energy released)

1. Describe and explain the shape of your graph.

Bubbles increase from 10 °C to about 40 °C — higher temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so enzyme–substrate collisions are more frequent. At 50 °C the rate crashes: the yeast enzymes are denatured — their active site shape is destroyed.

2. Where does the energy in anaerobic respiration come from, if there is no oxygen?

From the glucose itself — it is only partially broken down (ethanol still contains lots of chemical energy). That is why anaerobic respiration releases less energy per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration.

3. Give one human use of yeast’s anaerobic respiration.

Bread-making (CO₂ makes the dough rise) and brewing/biofuel (ethanol production).
